The Complete Guide to PDF Documents for Government Job Applications

Everything you need to know about photo, signature, and certificate size limits for SSC, IBPS, Railway RRB, UPSC and other Indian government exam portals — and how to prepare every file without uploading a single document anywhere.

Every recruitment cycle, lakhs of candidates finish filling out an SSC, IBPS, Railway RRB, or UPSC application form only to get stuck at the very last step: the upload page. The form itself was rarely the hard part — it's the strict, portal-specific file size limits on your photograph, signature, and supporting documents that trip people up. A scanned certificate that looks perfectly fine on your phone can be 4–5 MB, while the portal wants it under 200KB. A studio photograph might be a crisp 2MB JPEG when the form caps photos at 50KB, and a single rejected upload can mean starting the whole preparation process again with the deadline clock still running.

This guide walks through what most major Indian government and banking exams expect from your documents, why applications get stuck even when every other detail is correct, and how to prepare everything — photo, signature, and certificates — using tools that never send your files to a server. Since Aadhaar cards, marksheets, and category certificates carry sensitive personal data, that last part matters as much as getting the size right.

Why Your Application Gets Rejected Over File Size

Government recruitment portals are built to handle enormous concurrent traffic — sometimes crores of applicants across a single SSC or Railway cycle — on infrastructure that has to stay fast and affordable for everyone, including candidates on a slow 2G or 3G connection in a smaller town. That's the real reason behind the tight caps: a 200KB limit isn't arbitrary, it keeps upload times short and server storage manageable at that scale.

The practical result is that a large share of stuck or rejected applications aren't about eligibility at all — they're about a scanned document being too large, the wrong format (a PNG uploaded where JPEG is required), or the wrong pixel dimensions. A few patterns cause most of the failures:

  • Photos straight from a phone camera are typically 2–8 MB — nowhere close to the 20–50KB most exam portals ask for.
  • Scans from a mobile scanning app are often saved as high-resolution colour PDFs, easily 3–6 MB for a single page.
  • Signatures scanned along with the full A4 sheet, instead of cropped tightly, come out far larger than the 10–20KB most banking exams expect.
  • Combined uploads (like "all other documents in one file") get stuck because candidates don't know how to merge several scans into one PDF, let alone hit a size target afterward.

None of this reflects on the application itself — it's almost always a file-preparation problem, and it's fixable in a few minutes with the right sequence of steps.

Photo, Signature & Document Size Chart

Requirements vary by exam and can change between recruitment cycles, so treat this table as a general starting point and always confirm the exact figure against your official notification before uploading.

Exam / Portal Photo Signature Certificates
SSC (CGL, CHSL, MTS) 20–50 KB · JPEG · 3.5×4.5cm 10–30 KB · JPEG · ~4×2cm Typically 200–500 KB
IBPS / SBI (PO, Clerk) 20–50 KB · JPEG · 200×230px 10–20 KB · JPEG · 140×60px Usually under 500 KB
Railway RRB (NTPC, Group D) 20–50 KB · JPEG · 3.5×4.5cm 10–20 KB · JPEG · 3.5×1.5cm Typically 200–500 KB
UPSC (Civil Services) 20–300 KB · JPEG · 200×230px 20–300 KB · JPEG · 400×100px Varies by form
State PSCs & other portals Commonly 20–50 KB Commonly 10–20 KB Often 100–500 KB

A useful shortcut: Railway RRB and SSC currently ask for nearly identical photo specifications, so a photo resized for one usually works for the other. UPSC is the outlier — it allows a noticeably larger file size, so a photo prepared for UPSC will often need to be compressed further before it fits a banking or SSC form.

Step-by-Step: Preparing Every Document Before You Apply

  1. Start with a good source file. A well-lit, front-facing photo against a plain light background compresses far more cleanly than a shadowy or cluttered one, so you get better clarity at the same file size. For certificates, scan at around 150 DPI in greyscale rather than colour wherever the portal allows it — colour scans of plain text rarely add value and cost three to four times the file size.
  2. Compress the photo and signature separately. Use the Compress Image tool and aim toward the lower end of your target range rather than right up against the limit — a 45KB photo against a 50KB cap leaves no room for the portal's own re-processing to push it over. Crop the signature tightly to the ink itself before compressing; empty white space around a signature only adds file size.
  3. Compress each certificate PDF. Run every scanned certificate, marksheet, or ID proof through Compress PDF and pick the target size your notification specifies. If a document still won't clear the bar after compression, the original scan resolution was probably too high to begin with — rescanning at a lower DPI usually beats repeated aggressive compression, which starts to blur text.
  4. Convert anything that isn't already a PDF. If a certificate only exists as a photo of the physical document, run it through JPG to PDF first — most portals expect a proper PDF for supporting documents even when they accept JPEG for the photo and signature.
  5. Merge multi-document uploads into one file. Where a portal asks for "all other documents" as a single upload, use Merge PDF to combine the already-compressed files in the right order, then compress the merged result again if it has crept over the limit.
  6. Check the result before you upload. Open the compressed file and actually read it — confirm the certificate number, dates, and any seal or signature are still legible, not just that the file size number looks right. A file that technically meets the limit but is illegible can still get an application rejected at verification.
  7. Keep a labelled copy of everything. Save the final photo, signature, and each certificate with clear filenames in one folder. Most of these documents get reused across several applications in the same recruitment season, so you won't need to redo this work each time.

How to Combine Multiple Certificates Into One PDF

This is one of the more common places candidates get stuck, because it's really two problems stacked together: combining several separate scans into a single document, and then getting that combined document back under the size limit.

The order matters here. Compress each certificate individually first, then merge — not the other way round. Merging first and compressing the combined file afterward often produces worse results, because a compressor applied across one large multi-page file tends to apply a single blanket setting to every page. That can over-compress a page that was already small, like a one-line ID card, while under-compressing a dense certificate that needed more work. Compressing each document to its best individual quality first, then merging the already-small files, gives far more control over the final size and keeps every page as legible as possible.

Once merged, check the total against the limit. Some portals size-cap "all other documents" as one combined figure, while others expect each certificate to be its own separately-limited upload — read the notification's upload instructions carefully rather than assuming, since this single detail is where a lot of otherwise well-prepared applications go wrong. If the combined file is still oversized, running Compress PDF again on the merged file usually brings it in. And if a page needs to be dropped, reordered, or ended up upside-down after scanning, Reorder Pages and Rotate PDF fix that without redoing the whole merge.

Common Mistakes That Cost Candidates Their Application

A handful of avoidable mistakes account for most of the resubmitted or rejected uploads each recruitment cycle. None of them are difficult to fix once you know to look for them:

  • Accidentally uploading the original, uncompressed file after preparing a compressed copy separately — always confirm you're selecting the right file, especially when both versions share a similar name.
  • Compressing so aggressively that text becomes unreadable. A file that meets the size limit but can't be verified isn't really a valid upload.
  • Using a screenshot of a document instead of a proper scan — screenshots often carry a different aspect ratio and lower clarity, and some portals reject them outright.
  • Signing the physical signature sheet in capital letters or with a decorative signature that doesn't match the applicant's usual signature — several exams reject this regardless of file size.
  • Leaving a file in PNG format when the portal explicitly asks for JPEG, or the reverse — the extension has to match, not just look visually similar.
  • Waiting until the final hours before the deadline to discover a size mismatch, exactly when portal servers are under the heaviest load of the entire cycle.

Checking Your File Size Before You Upload

A surprising number of failed uploads come down to one thing: not knowing the actual file size before hitting submit. Checking takes a few seconds and saves a resubmission later.

On Android: open Files by Google (or your file manager), long-press the file, and the size appears in the details panel or a small info icon. Most manufacturer file managers show it the same way under "Properties" or "Details".

On iPhone: open the Files app, tap the "i" info button on the document, or swipe down on the file with three fingers in some iOS versions to reveal size and format at once.

On a computer: right-click the file and choose Properties on Windows, or Get Info on a Mac. Both show the exact size in KB or MB near the top of the panel.

Since every tool here works fully offline once the page has loaded, you can prepare and re-check every document from a phone at home or in a cyber café, without needing a laptop, a fast connection, or a second visit just to get the size right.

Why Offline Processing Matters for These Documents

Every document discussed here — Aadhaar card, category certificate, marksheet, bank passbook — carries information most people would rather not hand to an unfamiliar server, especially when a tool only needs to resize the file, not read what's inside it.

Tools that process files entirely in the browser, using WebAssembly to run the compression and conversion locally on your own device, never transmit the file anywhere. Nothing is uploaded, so there's nothing sitting on a remote server to be retained or repurposed later. It also means the tool keeps working through an unreliable connection — once the page has loaded, you can switch to airplane mode and the compression, merging, or conversion still completes, since your own device is doing the work rather than a server on the other end.

This is a genuinely different approach from most free PDF and photo tools, which upload your file to a server, process it there, and send the result back — a round trip that's often unnecessary for a task as simple as resizing or compressing. For a one-off task that doesn't involve personal data, the difference may not matter much. For a document that includes your Aadhaar number, date of birth, category, or family income details, it's worth knowing which kind of tool you're actually using before you upload anything.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I reduce PDF size to 100KB without uploading it anywhere?
Use the Compress PDF tool above and set 100KB as your target. The compression runs locally in your browser, so the file is never sent to a server — it works the same way whether you're online or not.
Is it safe to edit bank statements or Aadhaar cards using PDF Mechanic?
Yes. Every tool on this page runs client-side using WebAssembly. Sensitive documents like bank statements, Aadhaar cards, and category certificates are processed entirely on your device and never leave it.
What is the safest PDF size for most government job applications?
There's no single universal number, but 200KB is a reasonable starting target for certificates across most SSC, Railway, and banking exams, with photos and signatures needing to be smaller — typically 20–50KB and 10–20KB. Always confirm the exact figure in your specific notification, since it varies by portal and can change between cycles.
Does compressing a PDF affect its validity for verification?
No. Compression reduces file size, usually by lowering image resolution slightly — it doesn't alter the certificate number or any text on the document. Verification checks the information on the certificate, not the size it was uploaded at.
Can I use the same photo for SSC, Railway, and IBPS applications?
Often yes for SSC and Railway RRB, since they currently ask for very similar photo specifications. IBPS and banking exams use a different pixel dimension, so the same source photo usually needs to be resized separately for banking forms even if the file size range looks similar.
Why does my compressed PDF still fail the upload?
The three most common reasons: the file is still fractionally over the limit (try a lower target), the pixel dimensions don't match what the portal expects even though the size is fine, or the format is wrong — a PDF uploaded where JPEG is required, or the reverse.
How do I combine multiple certificates into one PDF under the size limit?
Compress each certificate individually first using Compress PDF, then combine the already-small files with Merge PDF. Compressing after merging tends to give worse, uneven results across pages.
What if my scanned certificate becomes illegible after compressing?
Rescan the original at a lower resolution — around 150 DPI, greyscale — rather than compressing a high-resolution colour scan down aggressively. A lower-resolution scan compressed lightly is almost always clearer than a high-resolution scan compressed hard to hit the same target size.
Can I reuse the same compressed documents across multiple exam applications?
Usually yes for certificates like marksheets, category, and income proof, as long as the target size and format match the new portal's requirements too. Photos and signatures need more care, since pixel dimensions differ between SSC, Railway, IBPS, and UPSC even when the KB range looks similar. Keeping one labelled folder per document type, sized for each exam you're applying to, saves having to redo this from scratch every time.

File size limits feel like a small, technical hurdle next to months of exam preparation, but they're also one of the few parts of the process you have complete control over. None of it needs design software or technical skill, just the right order of steps and a few minutes per document. Every tool linked above works the same way: open it, drop in your file, and download the result, with nothing ever leaving your device in between.
